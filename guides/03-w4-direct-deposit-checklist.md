# W-4 & Direct Deposit Sanity Checklist

*Two forms decide where your money goes all year: the W-4 (taxes) and the direct deposit form (your paycheck actually reaching you). Both are easy to fill out right — this checklist walks you through both in plain English.*

From **Asdale Dynamics, LLC**. No accounts, no data collection — this is you, on paper, checking your own work.

---

## Part A: The W-4, without the headache

The W-4 tells your employer how much federal tax to hold out of each paycheck. Fill it out *too little withheld* and you owe in April; *too much* and you gave the government a free loan. Either way you survive — but sane beats surprise.

### Before you start

- [ ] Have your Social Security number handy (it's on prior tax forms; no need to memorize)
- [ ] Know your filing status (Single, Married filing jointly, Head of Household...) — when unsure, see IRS.gov or a tax pro; this kit doesn't guess for you
- [ ] Count jobs: do you (or a spouse) have more than one job right now?

### Fill-in sanity checks

- [ ] Steps 1(a–b): legal name exactly as on your Social Security card (nicknames cause mismatch headaches)
- [ ] Step 1(c): filing status marked — exactly one box
- [ ] Step 2: if you have a **second job or a working spouse**, either check the box in 2(c) *or* use the IRS estimator — picking the wrong one is the #1 cause of April regret
- [ ] Step 3: only claim dependents you actually have; this *reduces* withholding
- [ ] Step 4(a): other income (interest, side gigs) — optional, improves accuracy
- [ ] Step 4(b): deductions — only if you itemize; most people leave blank
- [ ] Step 4(c): extra $ per paycheck — optional "safety cushion" if you like owing nothing in April
- [ ] You signed it. An unsigned W-4 is a decorative paper.
- [ ] You kept a copy or photo of the signed version (see Papers: Keep or Shred)

### Common settling-in mistakes

- Writing "Married" when the steadier choice for a second-job household is 2(c) — costs real money later
- Claiming exempt when you're not exempt (fun for months, painful in April)
- Forgetting to update your W-4 after a life change (marriage, new kid, second job)

## Part B: Direct deposit, the "measure twice" version

One flipped digit and your first paycheck becomes a two-week scavenger hunt. Ninety seconds of checking beats that 100% of the time.

### The checklist

- [ ] ★ Account number — read it aloud, digit by digit, against your check or banking app
- [ ] ★ Routing number — 9 digits; confirm it's the routing number for **your bank's ACH/direct deposit** (it's on a check's bottom-left, or in your banking app's "account details")
- [ ] Confirm the account is a **checking** account if the form assumes checking (savings works too, but match the account type to the form)
- [ ] If split deposits (part to savings, part to checking): the percentages/amounts add up to 100%
- [ ] Signed it, dated it
- [ ] You know when the first payday is, and you don't book rent around the *first* check being perfect — first paychecks sometimes arrive by paper check or a day late

### Fast fails (each of these happened to someone, somewhere)

- Transposing two digits of the account number
- Copying the routing number from a credit card (wrong — it's a card number)
- Leaving an old bank account active with no balance in it

## Part C: First-paycheck day check

When your first paycheck lands:

- [ ] Opening it isn't scary because it usually is right on one of two days — confirm the pay date and frequency (weekly / biweekly / semimonthly)
- [ ] Gross pay matches what you were told (offer letter math: salary ÷ pay periods per year)
- [ ] Deposit landed in the account you chose
- [ ] Deductions look sane: federal tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), any benefits you actually elected (if something's deducted that you never signed up for, ask payroll — it's a normal question)
- [ ] Your chosen withholding matches your plan roughly (a tax withholding *estimator* on IRS.gov can sanity-check)
- [ ] Keep this pay stub for at least a year (see Papers: Keep or Shred)

If something looks wrong: ask payroll **in writing** (email), same week. First-cycle mistakes are common and fixable, but only when people speak up early.
